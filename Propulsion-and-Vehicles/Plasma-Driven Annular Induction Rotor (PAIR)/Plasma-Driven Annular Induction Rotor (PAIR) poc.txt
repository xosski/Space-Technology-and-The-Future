Abstract

The Plasma-Driven Annular Induction Rotor (PAIR) is a proposed energy-recovery device intended to investigate whether a portion of the kinetic and pressure energy contained within a flowing plasma stream can be converted into useful electrical power through a mechanically rotating electromagnetic stage.

PAIR consists conceptually of one or more annular rotors positioned within a plasma-flow chamber. Each rotor incorporates curved, cupped, or helical flow-interaction surfaces designed to convert axial plasma-flow momentum into rotational motion. The rotating assembly interacts magnetically with stationary induction coils positioned outside or thermally isolated from the principal plasma channel.

The proposed energy pathway is:

Plasma Flow→Rotor Torque→Rotation→Changing Magnetic Flux→Electrical Output
	​


Multiple PAIR stages may be arranged sequentially, including counter-rotating configurations intended to investigate staged energy extraction and downstream swirl management.

PAIR does not assume that ionized gas constitutes an independent energy source. Electrical generation must correspond to energy removed from the flowing plasma/gas system. Consequently, the principal POC objective is to determine whether useful electrical energy can be recovered while maintaining acceptable downstream plasma characteristics and pressure losses.

The proposed proof of concept therefore focuses on measurable quantities: plasma/gas mass flow, pressure differential, temperature, rotor speed, torque, electrical output, downstream plasma properties, and complete system energy balance.

1. Concept

Plasma-generation and plasma-conditioning systems can produce moving ionized gases containing thermal, pressure, kinetic, electromagnetic, and chemical energy.

In many proposed systems, the plasma stream subsequently enters another processing stage or leaves the apparatus carrying residual energy.

PAIR investigates whether a rotating annular structure directly within that flow can recover a portion of its available kinetic/pressure energy.

8. The Central PAIR Hypothesis

The POC hypothesis can be stated in falsifiable form:

A cupped annular rotor positioned within a controlled plasma/gas flow can convert measurable flow energy into rotational mechanical energy and subsequently electrical energy while preserving sufficient downstream flow/plasma performance to provide a useful system-level energy-recovery function.

A second hypothesis concerns staged operation:

Multiple independently loaded PAIR stages can distribute energy extraction more effectively than a single equivalent rotor while providing controllable downstream angular momentum and pressure characteristics.

Neither hypothesis should be considered established until experimentally demonstrated.

15. PAIR versus Direct Electromagnetic Extraction

PAIR should not be evaluated in isolation.

Because the working fluid is ionized, a competing approach may be some form of direct electromagnetic/MHD extraction without a mechanical rotor.

Conversely, if PAIR performs substantially better under the relevant plasma conditions, that becomes experimentally meaningful.

18. Thermal and Materials Challenges

PAIR would face substantially harsher conditions than an ordinary low-temperature turbine.

Potential problems include:

plasma-facing material erosion,
electrode/rotor sputtering,
thermal expansion,
bearing failure,
magnetic material temperature limits,
electrical arcing,
dielectric breakdown,
electromagnetic interference,
rotor imbalance,
plasma-induced surface charging,
and contamination of the plasma stream.

Consequently, an eventual practical implementation might require the electromagnetic components to remain physically outside the hottest region.

Magnetic bearings or contactless coupling could eventually be investigated, but they should not be prerequisites for the first POC.

19. POC Success Criteria

PAIR should be considered experimentally promising only if the test demonstrates all of the following:

Repeatable rotor acceleration produced by the controlled flow.
Repeatable mechanical power measurement.
Repeatable electrical generation under a defined load.
Electrical output scaling predictably with operating conditions.
Energy conservation within experimental uncertainty.
Quantified pressure/flow penalty.
Stable operation for a meaningful test duration.
No unacceptable downstream plasma degradation.
A measurable advantage over the fixed-obstruction control.
Sufficient net benefit to justify additional mechanical complexity.

A negative result would also be valuable.

20. Recommended Development Sequence

The safest and most informative progression is:

Phase I — Cold-flow rotor test. Demonstrate the cupped annular geometry, characterize RPM, torque and pressure drop without plasma.

Phase II — Generator integration. Add magnetic rotor/stator coupling and obtain calibrated electrical output versus generator load.

Phase III — Low-energy plasma test. Determine whether ionization materially changes rotor behavior, losses, electrical isolation, erosion or electromagnetic interaction.

Phase IV — Multi-stage PAIR. Compare single, same-direction and counter-rotating arrays.

Phase V — DPCM integration. Determine whether conditioned plasma changes PAIR performance.

Phase VI — System energy balance. Measure the entire apparatus from source electrical/fuel input to downstream useful output and recovered PAIR electricity.

Only after Phase VI would claims concerning improved system efficiency be justified.

22. Conclusion

The Plasma-Driven Annular Induction Rotor (PAIR) proposes a mechanically rotating energy-recovery stage located within a flowing plasma or high-energy gas stream.

Its defining feature is a cupped or helical annular rotor that converts part of the stream's momentum into rotation while a magnetically coupled generator converts that rotation into electrical power.

The architecture permits single-stage, multi-stage and counter-rotating implementations and may additionally permit electrically controlled flow loading through variation of generator torque.

PAIR does not propose creation of additional energy. Its feasibility depends upon whether recoverable electrical output exceeds the practical penalties associated with pressure loss, downstream plasma disturbance, thermal loading, rotor drag, electromagnetic losses and mechanical complexity.

Accordingly, PAIR is well suited to a TRL 1–2 experimental program because its fundamental proposition can be tested without constructing the larger propulsion architecture.

The decisive experiment is straightforward:

Measure everything entering the PAIR stage, everything leaving it, and the electricity recovered between them.
	​


If that balance demonstrates useful recovery while preserving the downstream conditions required by the larger system, PAIR becomes a candidate subsystem rather than merely a conceptual rotor.

Proposed designation:

PAIR-01 — Plasma-Driven Annular Induction Rotor: Experimental Plasma-Flow Energy-Recovery Stage
