PTEC-SR
Superconducting Rotor - Proof-of-Concept White Paper
Concept status: exploratory engineering architecture, Version 0.1
Abstract
PTEC-SR is a proposed contactless rotating energy core built around superconducting magnetic bearing support, a
magnetically coupled rotor, stator coils, vacuum or low-conductivity isolation, and a cryogenic subsystem. The purpose of
the proof of concept is not to claim net energy creation, but to test whether very low mechanical bearing loss, stable
high-speed rotation, and useful bidirectional electromagnetic coupling can coexist in a compact module. The rotor can
store rotational energy, accept externally supplied mechanical or electromagnetic energy, and return part of that stored
energy electrically through conventional induction.
1. Core Thesis
The PTEC-SR replaces conventional contact bearings with a superconducting magnetic bearing (SMB) arrangement. A
levitated rotor is surrounded by stator coils used for motor/generator coupling. Thermal isolation separates the cold
bearing region from any warm or plasma-facing region. In a larger VAPS/PTEC architecture, the rotor would function as
an energy buffer and conversion stage rather than as a primary energy source.
Conservation boundary: Every electrical output term must be traceable to stored rotor energy or an external prime
input. Magnetic levitation and superconductivity reduce losses; they do not supply continuous free energy.
2. Proposed Architecture
Subsystem POC function
PTEC rotor core Stores angular momentum; carries magnetic field structure.
Superconducting magnetic bearing Provides contactless support and passive/active stabilization.
Stator coil array Accelerates/brakes rotor and extracts electrical energy by changing magnetic flux.
Vacuum / low-conductivity enclosure Reduces aerodynamic drag and thermal conduction.
Cryogenic loop Maintains the superconducting bearing below its operating temperature.
Thermal barrier Protects the cold stage from warm engine/plasma-side heat loads.
Power electronics Controls motor/generator operation and interfaces with a DC bus/storage.
Sensors + controller Monitors position, speed, temperature, vibration, field strength, and quench state.
3. Operating Principle
1) Cool the superconducting bearing assembly below its required operating temperature. 2) Establish the magnetic field
configuration and levitate/center the rotor. 3) Use stator coils or another external prime mover to accelerate the rotor. 4)
Hold rotational energy with minimized bearing and windage losses. 5) When power is requested, load the stator
electrically; the resulting electromagnetic torque slows the rotor while generating electrical output. 6) Recharge by
reversing the power flow and operating the stator as a motor.

The basic relationships are conventional: rotational energy E = 1/2 I omega^2; mechanical power P = tau omega; and
induced EMF follows the rate of change of magnetic flux. Useful round-trip efficiency is therefore bounded below 100%
after stator, inverter, cryogenic, magnetic, aerodynamic, and control losses.

5. Test Program
Phase 1 - Static levitation: characterize lift, restoring force, thermal stability, and positional drift with a non-rotating
mass.
Phase 2 - Low-speed rotor: validate balancing, emergency touchdown bearings, sensing, vacuum enclosure, and
controlled spin-down.
Phase 3 - Loss mapping: measure decay rate versus pressure, temperature, RPM, and magnetic configuration to
separate bearing, windage, eddy-current, and electrical losses.
Phase 4 - Motor/generator coupling: measure electrical input during acceleration and recovered electrical energy
during controlled deceleration. Report round-trip efficiency from calibrated DC-bus measurements.
Phase 5 - Thermal integration: introduce representative external heat loads while maintaining cryogenic stability. A
plasma-facing experiment, if pursued, should remain a separate later-stage subsystem until the rotor is independently
validated.
6. Instrumentation and Falsifiable Success Criteria
Minimum instrumentation should include rotor RPM, three-axis displacement, vibration, stator voltage/current, DC-bus
power, bearing and shield temperatures, vacuum pressure, cryocooler input power, and containment/interlock state. A
useful POC succeeds if it demonstrates repeatable levitation, controlled acceleration/deceleration, quantified low-loss
spin-down, stable thermal operation, and a measured energy balance that closes within instrument uncertainty.
A particularly important figure of merit is whole-system round-trip efficiency: recovered DC energy divided by DC
energy used to charge the rotor, with cryogenic and control-system consumption explicitly included. This prevents a
visually impressive low-friction rotor from being mistaken for an energetically favorable complete system.
7. Vehicle / VAPS Integration Hypothesis
Within the broader VAPS concept, PTEC-SR can be treated as a bidirectional buffer: external energy -> power electronics
-> stator -> rotor, and rotor -> stator -> DC bus -> traction/storage. If a plasma or vapor subsystem is investigated, its
contribution should be measured as an input or conversion process at the same system boundary. The PTEC-SR
architecture does not by itself make a vehicle fossil-free; that depends on the upstream energy source and total lifecycle
energy path.
8. Principal Engineering Risks
Risk POC mitigation
Rotor burst / imbalance Conservative RPM progression, precision balancing, remote operation, rated containment.
Superconductor quench Temperature/field monitoring, automatic shutdown, energy dump path.
Magnetic instability Active sensing/control and independent touchdown bearings.
Cryogenic parasitic load Measure cryocooler input explicitly; optimize thermal isolation.
Eddy-current / hysteresis lossMaterial selection and loss mapping across speed/field conditions.
Thermal ingress Radiation shields, vacuum gap, ceramic/composite thermal breaks.
Overstated energy claims Closed energy accounting with calibrated instruments and uncertainty bounds.

9. Decision Gate
The concept should advance only after the bench system demonstrates a favorable, reproducible loss budget and safe
rotor dynamics. If cryogenic power plus electromagnetic losses overwhelm the bearing-loss advantage, the PTEC-SR
may still be useful as a specialized flywheel or research platform, but not as an efficiency-improving vehicle energy
system.
10. Conclusion
PTEC-SR is physically plausible as a superconducting-magnetic-bearing flywheel/generator research architecture. Its
strongest testable proposition is not extraordinary energy production, but whether contactless support and controlled
electromagnetic coupling can deliver sufficiently low losses to justify the added cryogenic, containment, and control
complexity. The next rational artifact is a low-energy benchtop rotor with complete instrumentation and a closed energy
ledger.
