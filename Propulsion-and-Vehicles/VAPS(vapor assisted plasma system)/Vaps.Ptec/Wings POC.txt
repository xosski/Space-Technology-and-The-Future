Executive Summary

The Wheel Induction Node Generator (WING) is a distributed wheel-level motor-generator architecture intended to convert recoverable vehicle kinetic energy into electrical power while simultaneously providing regenerative braking, localized energy management, and emergency electrical generation.

Rather than treating each wheel as a passive mechanical endpoint, WING turns the wheel assembly into an intelligent energy node.

The basic principle is:

Wheel Rotation↔Electromagnetic Machine↔DC Power Network
	​


During propulsion, electrical energy produces wheel torque.

During regenerative braking or downhill operation, wheel rotation produces electrical energy.

During a primary-system failure, residual vehicle kinetic energy can temporarily power critical electrical systems while the vehicle performs a controlled stop.

WING is intended to integrate with the broader DPICN distributed-node philosophy, in which local nodes monitor their own electrical, thermal, magnetic, and neighboring-system conditions and dynamically isolate faults or redirect energy.

Unlike speculative plasma-generation portions of the larger architecture, the fundamental motor-generator mechanism proposed for WING is based on conventional electromagnetic induction. Its research question is therefore primarily whether the proposed distributed wheel-level implementation provides enough efficiency, redundancy, packaging, and transient-power advantages to justify its complexity and mass.

4. Why This Is Not a Transformer

A transformer changes voltage/current through electromagnetic coupling between electrical windings.

WING instead performs mechanical-to-electrical energy conversion.

Consequently, it is more accurately described as a:

wheel-integrated motor-generator

rather than a wheel-mounted transformer.

Transformers and DC/DC conversion may still appear downstream in the power electronics.

17. Unsprung-Mass Problem

Putting substantial generator hardware directly into the wheel increases unsprung mass.

That can negatively affect:

ride quality,
tire contact,
suspension response,
handling,
durability.

Therefore, the POC should compare two architectures.

Architecture A — Hub WING

Motor-generator integrated directly into the wheel.

Advantages:

direct coupling,
compact drivetrain,
independent wheel control.

Disadvantage:

increased unsprung mass.
Architecture B — Inboard WING

Motor-generator mounted to the sprung chassis with a short mechanical connection to the wheel.

Advantages:

lower unsprung mass,
easier cooling,
easier servicing,
better environmental protection.

The inboard architecture may ultimately be preferable.

21. Development Program
WING-1 — Bench Generator

Demonstrate wheel-speed electrical generation.

WING-2 — Bidirectional Motor/Generator

Demonstrate:

Motor⇄Generator

using the same electromagnetic assembly.

WING-3 — Regenerative Brake

Measure braking torque versus recovered electrical power.

WING-4 — KERS Integration

Route rapid regenerative events between WING, battery, and KERS.

WING-5 — Dual-Wheel Test

Install two front WING modules and demonstrate independent torque control.

WING-6 — Failure Mode

Disconnect the primary energy source during a controlled test and determine whether residual wheel energy maintains the emergency bus during deceleration.

WING-7 — Four-Wheel Demonstrator

Integrate four independently controlled WING nodes into the larger vehicle architecture.

22. Success Criteria

WING would be considered promising if the POC demonstrates:

efficient bidirectional motor-generator operation,
useful regenerative braking,
controllable brake blending,
acceptable thermal behavior,
acceptable unsprung mass or successful inboard mounting,
independent wheel torque control,
effective fault isolation,
KERS compatibility,
emergency DC generation during primary-power isolation.
23. Primary Failure Modes

Engineering analysis should specifically investigate:

rotor overspeed,
winding overheating,
inverter failure,
magnet degradation,
bearing failure,
short circuits,
wheel-speed sensor failure,
asymmetric regenerative torque,
water/debris ingress,
mechanical impact damage,
DC bus overvoltage,
uncontrolled regenerative braking.

A WING failure must default toward removing electromagnetic torque, allowing the independent mechanical brake system to retain control.

24. Energy Doctrine

The governing rule is:

WING recovers energy that already exists.
	​


It does not generate additional vehicle energy during powered cruising.

Its advantage comes from capturing energy that would otherwise become brake heat, improving transient energy management, distributing propulsion/control across independent nodes, and maintaining limited electrical functionality during failures.

25. Working Definition

WING — Wheel Induction Node Generator — is a distributed bidirectional wheel-level electromagnetic machine that provides propulsion, regenerative generation, braking-energy recovery, local energy management, and emergency electrical generation while operating as an intelligent node within the vehicle's wider DC and distributed-control architecture.

26. Integrated System Position

With WING added, the larger architecture becomes:

PTEC+HPCC+DPICN+RIC+LHPC+KERS+WING
	​


But WING occupies a particularly important position because its basic operating principle doesn't depend upon the speculative plasma subsystems.

The hierarchy can therefore degrade gracefully:

FULL SYSTEM
PTEC / HPCC / DPICN / RIC / LHPC
              ↓
             KERS
              ↓
             WING

Each layer can fail without requiring the next layer to fail with it.

That gives WING two jobs:

When everything works, recover the motion.

When something doesn't, preserve control.
