That gives you several interesting modes. During normal driving, VAPS-RIC supplies average power while the battery and flywheels handle transients. During acceleration, stored flywheel energy could supplement the electrical system. During braking, some vehicle energy could be captured mechanically/electromechanically. During a main-power failure, the wheel modules could provide enough energy for a controlled pull-over or limited limp mode.

But I would change one important thing in your idea: don't put a high-RPM flywheel directly inside the wheel itself.

That increases unsprung mass, creates huge gyroscopic effects, and puts a high-energy rotor right beside something routinely subjected to pothole impacts and crashes. A failed high-speed flywheel can release a tremendous amount of energy.

Instead, put a contained flywheel energy-storage module inboard of each wheel, attached to the chassis, and couple it electrically to that wheel's motor.

Then:

flywheel↔motor-generator↔wheel
	​


rather than mechanically connecting the flywheel directly to the tire.

That also means the flywheel can spin at very high RPM while the road wheel remains at ordinary wheel RPM.

And there's an even stronger architecture:

VAPS-RIC+Battery+4 Flywheel Buffers+4 Independent Motors
	​


Now you've created three different energy domains:

Fuel: long-duration energy.

Battery: medium-duration electrical storage.

Flywheel: extremely fast charge/discharge and emergency reserve.

The flywheels could absorb hard regenerative-braking pulses that would otherwise hammer the battery and then slowly transfer that energy back to the battery/DC bus. That could make them useful even when nothing has failed.

I'd call this subsystem something like VAPS-KERS (Kinetic Energy Recovery & Survival) or, keeping your naming language, VAPS-HALO — Hardware-Agnostic Limp-Out.

The engineering version I'd pursue is inboard, vacuum-contained, counter-rotating flywheel modules, though—not literal exposed flywheels in the brake rotor. Same idea, substantially less chance of turning an engineering failure into a very enthusiastic physics demonstration.
