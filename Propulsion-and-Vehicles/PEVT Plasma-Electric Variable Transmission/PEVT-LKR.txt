Abstract

PEVT-LKR (Plasma-Electric Variable Transmission — Linear Kinetic Recovery) is a proposed electromechanical energy-recovery architecture in which mechanical energy from a rotating drivetrain is periodically transferred into a guided linear magnetic translator, temporarily stored as translator kinetic energy, and subsequently recovered as electrical energy through stationary induction windings.

The concept introduces a controllable mechanical coupling between the PEVT drive system and a free-moving linear translator.

Rather than maintaining permanent mechanical engagement, the translator operates through distinct charge, coast, recovery, and reset/re-engagement states.

The proposed energy pathway is:

Rotational Mechanical Energy→Linear Kinetic Energy→Electromagnetic Induction→Conditioned Electrical Energy

PEVT-LKR does not propose creation or amplification of energy.

Instead, it investigates whether kinetic energy can be temporarily shifted out of the primary drivetrain, stored mechanically in linear motion, and subsequently recovered in a controlled manner to provide transient electrical power, regenerative-energy capture, load smoothing, or other useful transmission behavior.

The central proof-of-concept question is:

Can a mechanically charged, subsequently decoupled magnetic translator deliver repeatable and controllable electrical energy to a load with sufficient round-trip efficiency, response speed, and power density to provide a useful transient-energy function within PEVT?

The proposed POC deliberately separates this question from plasma operation.

2. Core Hypothesis

PEVT-LKR tests two related hypotheses.

H1 — Energy Recovery

A mechanical drivetrain can transfer measurable energy into a guided magnetic translator, after which the translator can be mechanically decoupled and its remaining kinetic energy converted into measurable electrical energy through electromagnetic induction.

H2 — System-Level Utility

Controlling when this stored energy is recovered may provide useful transient electrical power or drivetrain load management compared with an otherwise identical PEVT configuration without LKR.

15. Relationship to VAPS-KERS

VAPS-KERS proposes storing kinetic energy in flywheels and subsequently returning that energy through motor-generator hardware for transient or emergency operation.

PEVT-LKR applies a related principle in a different mechanical geometry:

VAPS-KERS=rotational kinetic storage

while:

PEVT-LKR=linear kinetic storage

This creates an experimentally interesting comparison.

A conventional flywheel may ultimately prove superior. If so, that is a legitimate result.

The relevant question is whether linear storage provides some compensating advantage in integration, response, electromagnetic extraction, packaging, synchronization, or PEVT operation.

21. Engineering Risks

Mechanical containment: A rapidly moving translator contains substantial kinetic energy and requires positive containment and controlled end-of-travel behavior.

Coupling shock: Rapid engagement with a moving drivetrain could produce damaging impulse loads.

Electromagnetic braking: High electrical extraction produces reaction force and may decelerate the translator much faster than expected.

Thermal loading: Coil resistance, eddy currents, power electronics and repeated cycling produce heat.

Magnetic degradation: Permanent magnets can lose performance through excessive temperature or unfavorable field conditions.

Guide wear: Linear bearing friction or misalignment may dominate coast-down losses.

Control failure: Mistimed engagement or extraction could introduce drivetrain shock.

Efficiency: Multiple conversions—

rotation→linear motion→electricity

—may make the architecture less efficient than direct electrical storage or a conventional rotary flywheel.

That possibility should be treated as a central experimental question rather than an afterthought.

22. Safety Architecture

The first POC should operate at deliberately low stored kinetic energy.

The translator requires:

physical containment;
redundant position sensing;
mechanical end stops;
controlled electromagnetic braking;
overspeed/overtravel detection;
emergency energy-dump load;
electrical isolation;
thermal shutdown;
guarded rotating coupling hardware.

A high-energy implementation should only follow successful low-energy characterization and formal mechanical safety analysis.

23. Success Criteria

PEVT-LKR should not be considered successful merely because the translator moves or the coil produces voltage.

A successful first POC requires:

repeatable mechanical charging;
controlled mechanical release;
measurable free-coast behavior;
predictable electromagnetic deceleration;
calibrated electrical energy delivered to a known load;
closed input/output energy accounting;
repeatable charge/recovery cycles;
quantified round-trip efficiency;
stable operation within declared mechanical and thermal limits.

A system-level PEVT-LKR demonstration additionally requires a measurable benefit relative to PEVT without LKR.

24. Failure Criteria

The architecture should be considered unsupported for its intended application if testing shows that:

η
round-trip
	​


is prohibitively low;

or if coupling losses, friction, heat, vibration, mass, wear or control complexity overwhelm the transient-power benefit.

Likewise, if a conventional flywheel, capacitor, battery, or direct motor-generator architecture provides equivalent functionality with substantially better performance, the linear architecture would require another measurable advantage to justify further development.

This comparison is essential to making the POC scientifically meaningful.

28. Research Questions for External Review

The first external engineering review should concentrate on the following questions:

1. What rotary-to-linear coupling architecture can transfer energy with the lowest shock and loss?

2. What translator mass/velocity combination provides useful transient energy without creating unreasonable containment requirements?

3. What magnet/coil geometry provides the best electrical recovery while minimizing eddy-current and thermal losses?

4. Can generator loading provide sufficiently fast and stable control of translator deceleration?

5. How quickly does the translator lose energy during coast?

6. Does linear kinetic storage provide any measurable advantage over rotary flywheel storage?

7. Can multiple LKR modules be phase-offset to provide smoother continuous output?

8. Does integrating LKR actually improve PEVT transient behavior once every charging and recovery loss is included?

30. Conclusion

PEVT-LKR — Linear Kinetic Recovery converts the proposed free-moving piston concept into a bounded electromechanical experiment.

Its operating doctrine is:

Engage→Accelerate→Decouple→Coast→Recover→Reset
	​


Mechanical energy from the PEVT drivetrain accelerates a magnetic translator. The translator is subsequently decoupled, allowing it to continue moving under its stored kinetic energy. Controlled electromagnetic loading then converts part of that kinetic energy into electrical output.

The architecture does not depend upon plasma, extraordinary electromagnetic effects, or energy amplification for its initial validation.

Its first experiment can therefore be performed entirely as a conventional electromechanical system.
