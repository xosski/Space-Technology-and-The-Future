PEVT: Plasma-Electric Variable Transmission

Concept Classification: Experimental Electromechanical / Plasma-Electric Energy Conversion System
Development Stage: Concept / TRL 1–2
Architecture Family: TCPPE / PTEC / PPCM
Date: August 2026

I. Abstract

The Plasma-Electric Variable Transmission (PEVT) is a proposed experimental energy-conversion architecture combining conventional mechanical power transmission with electrically excited plasma and electromagnetic conditioning stages.

The central concept is analogous to a mechanical transmission, but instead of using gears alone to modify the relationship between input speed and output torque, PEVT investigates whether a controlled sequence of:

mechanical rotation → electrical generation → plasma/electromagnetic conditioning → electrical or mechanical recovery

can provide useful variable energy-transfer characteristics.

The proposed proof of concept consists of mechanically coupled rotating stages, electrically driven plasma or discharge modules, a Plasma/Electric Conditioning stage (PTEC), and instrumentation capable of independently measuring mechanical, electrical, plasma, magnetic, and thermal energy flows.

PEVT does not assume creation of energy, reactionless propulsion, or greater-than-unity operation.

Its primary research question is considerably narrower:

Can an actively conditioned plasma/electromagnetic intermediate stage measurably alter the efficiency, torque-speed behavior, transient response, controllability, or recoverable electrical energy of a mechanically coupled electromechanical transmission?

The POC is designed so that this hypothesis can be experimentally falsified.

The associated TCPPE architecture also describes dual coils, plasma channels, bipolar modulators, and feedback between plasma/electrical subsystems.

PEVT introduces an additional subsystem:

IV. Core Hypothesis

The PEVT proof of concept tests the hypothesis that an electrically controlled plasma or ionized-gas region inserted into an electromechanical energy-conversion architecture may modify measurable system behavior.

Possible measurable effects include changes in:

electrical impedance;
inductive coupling;
electromagnetic phase relationships;
generator loading;
motor loading;
torque versus rotational velocity;
transient response;
electrical energy recovery;
plasma stability;
thermal losses;
conversion efficiency.

No particular effect is assumed in advance.

The null hypothesis is:

H
0
	​

:PTEC/plasma activation produces no useful improvement over the control system.

The experimental hypothesis is:

H

:PTEC/plasma activation produces a reproducible measurable change in one or more system parameters.

The distinction is intentional: PEVT succeeds as an experiment even if the proposed effect is disproven.

Rather than permanently assigning the two elements positive and negative polarity, the POC should permit multiple excitation modes.

These could include opposite-polarity excitation, alternating excitation, phase-offset AC excitation, pulsed excitation, and reference-electrode operation.

This allows the experiment to investigate whether different electrical boundary conditions produce significantly different transmission behavior.

VII. PTEC Conditioning Stage

PTEC acts as the control layer between electrical generation and the plasma interaction region.

For purposes of the PEVT experiment, PTEC does not need to demonstrate exotic energy amplification.

Its initial purpose is simply to control measurable electrical parameters.

These include voltage, current, frequency, phase, pulse timing, polarity, duty cycle, and discharge operating conditions.

This converts the conceptual "super-conditioning" stage into something experimentally definable:

PTEC controls the state of the plasma-electric interface while PEVT measures its consequences.

VIII. Stage C — Secondary Conversion Stage

The secondary stage attempts to recover useful electrical or mechanical energy from the coupled system.

Possible implementations could eventually investigate induction-based conversion, conventional motor-generator conversion, or plasma/electromagnetic interaction.

For the earliest POC, conventional hardware should be preferred wherever possible.

This is important because conventional equipment provides known efficiency curves and therefore gives the plasma stage a meaningful baseline.

The experiment is not:

"Can this apparatus rotate?"

The relevant question is:

"Does activation and conditioning of the plasma stage change the measured behavior of an otherwise characterized transmission?"

IX. Mechanical Coupling

The mechanical transmission loop is the defining addition introduced by PEVT.

A chain, belt, geared coupling, or common shaft mechanically links the rotating conversion stages.

Instrumentation should measure rotational velocity and torque on both sides of the system.

Mechanical power can then be calculated:

This allows researchers to determine whether changes observed electrically correspond to genuine mechanical changes rather than measurement artifacts elsewhere in the circuit.

The drive mechanism also provides rotational synchronization between conversion stages.

Thus the mechanical transmission acts simultaneously as a power-transfer mechanism and experimental timing reference.

X. Variable Transmission Concept

The "variable" portion of PEVT does not initially imply a conventional continuously variable transmission ratio.

Instead, PEVT proposes an electronically adjustable energy-transfer characteristic.

Changing PTEC parameters could theoretically alter the operating point of the coupled system.

Define a control vector:

C=[V, I, f, ϕ, D, B, p]

where approximately:

V=voltage
I=current
f=excitation frequency
ϕ=phase offset
D=pulse duty cycle
B=magnetic field
p=gas pressure

PEVT then asks whether changing C changes the measurable transmission state:

C→{T,ω,P,η,thermal loss}

in a reproducible manner.

That relationship would constitute the PEVT "transmission map."

XV. Success Criteria

PEVT should not define success as merely producing plasma, rotating a shaft, or producing voltage.

Those phenomena are already expected.

A successful POC would instead demonstrate a statistically meaningful and repeatable alteration of transmission behavior attributable specifically to the conditioned plasma/electromagnetic stage.

Evidence should survive:

repeated runs;
randomized activation/deactivation;
matched control experiments;
sensor calibration;
complete input/output power accounting;
uncertainty analysis.

Ideally, an independent laboratory should be capable of reproducing the result.

XVI. Failure Criteria

The PEVT hypothesis should be considered unsupported under tested conditions if:

∣Δη∣≤measurement uncertainty

or if observed changes can be explained by ordinary heating, friction, electromagnetic interference with instrumentation, altered mechanical loading, power-supply behavior, or other uncontrolled variables.

A negative result would still constrain future TCPPE/PTEC development.

XVII. Engineering Risks

The principal near-term risks are not speculative physics but conventional engineering problems.

High-voltage discharge introduces arcing and insulation challenges.

Plasma systems introduce thermal loading and material erosion.

Rapidly changing electromagnetic fields can contaminate sensor measurements.

Mechanical rotating assemblies introduce vibration and failure hazards.

The interaction of high voltage, plasma, rotating machinery, and stored electrical energy means the initial prototype belongs in a properly equipped laboratory environment rather than an improvised high-power setup.

XVIII. Development Roadmap

Phase I — Simulation and analytical model

Develop a complete energy-flow model and determine expected baseline losses.

Phase II — Mechanical reference transmission

Build and characterize the motor/generator transmission without plasma.

Phase III — Static plasma experiment

Characterize the proposed PTEC/plasma subsystem independently of rotating machinery.

Phase IV — Low-power integration

Couple PTEC/plasma operation to the instrumented transmission.

Phase V — Parameter sweep

Generate the first experimental:

T(ω,C)

and

η(ω,C)

maps.

Phase VI — Independent replication

Provide apparatus geometry, operating parameters, calibration procedures, raw measurements, and analysis code to an external laboratory.

Only after reproducible results should higher-power aerospace applications be considered.

XIX. Potential Research Relevance

If a measurable effect exists, PEVT could potentially warrant further investigation for electromechanical energy management, electrically variable transmission behavior, plasma-assisted power conversion, regenerative systems, high-power aerospace powertrains, or integrated plasma/electromagnetic systems.

These are potential research directions rather than demonstrated applications.

The immediate value of PEVT is therefore not a claim that it replaces an automotive or spacecraft transmission.

Its value is that it creates a controlled experimental platform for answering whether the proposed coupling mechanism does anything useful at all.

XX. Conclusion

The Plasma-Electric Variable Transmission (PEVT) converts a broader speculative plasma-power concept into a bounded experimental question.

The architecture combines a conventional mechanical transmission with electrical generation, controlled plasma excitation, electromagnetic conditioning, and energy recovery.

Rather than presuming extraordinary performance, PEVT is designed around comparative measurement:

Baseline→Plasma→Conditioned Plasma
	​


If conditioning produces no measurable benefit, the hypothesis can be rejected or revised.

If a reproducible difference appears, its magnitude, mechanism, efficiency, and scaling behavior can then be investigated independently.

That makes PEVT particularly useful inside the larger GhostCore architecture: it converts the idea from "a plasma transmission should work" into "here is the apparatus capable of proving whether it works."
