Conceptually, yes — there’s a real aerodynamic idea hiding inside what you’re describing. I’d frame it less as an “X-wing emergency mode” and more as a deployable emergency high-lift / stability architecture.

One correction first: a powerless fixed-wing aircraft is already a glider. The problem in an engine-out or stall event is not “making it glide at all,” but improving lift-to-drag ratio, lowering stall speed, preserving controllability, and keeping the aircraft inside a recoverable angle-of-attack envelope. NASA’s high-angle-of-attack research repeatedly emphasizes that loss of lateral/directional control near stall is often the real killer, not simply lack of thrust.

Your retractable half/third-length wing extensions could plausibly help if they act like deployable span/area extensions. More wing area generally lowers wing loading, which can reduce the speed required to generate a given amount of lift. NASA has studied emergency/high-lift flap concepts specifically intended to reduce stall speed, though the relationship between flap geometry, angle of attack, and stall behavior is not trivial.

The part I would change is the inverted scoop concept. If those scoops mainly catch airflow, they become drag devices. That can absolutely reduce speed, but slowing an already-stalled aircraft is usually the opposite of what you need. A stalled wing needs the angle of attack reduced and adequate airflow restored. Large drag devices could be useful later in the emergency sequence—especially during descent or landing—but deploying them aggressively at the instant of a stall could make the energy state worse.

A more aerodynamically useful version of your “scoop” would be something closer to a deployable slat, slot, or carefully shaped leading-edge element:

NORMAL FLIGHT


      airflow ---->


        _________
   ____/         \____
  /                   \




EMERGENCY DEPLOYMENT


         deployable leading-edge/slat
                    ↓
          ___       ___
   ______/   \_____/   \________
  /                           \
              ↑
       extra effective wing area

Instead of simply resisting the air, it manipulates the boundary layer so the wing can continue producing useful lift at a higher angle of attack or lower airspeed.

The really interesting part of your concept is deploying different surfaces independently.

Imagine four emergency surfaces:

Each could have its own actuator and sensor feedback.

Then the system isn't merely “extend everything.”

It can do:

→ reduce deployment on opposite side

→ differential drag / vertical control correction

→ reduce effective AoA
→ deploy appropriate forward/high-lift surfaces
→ preserve airflow


rapid descent but wings flying
→ transition to symmetric glide configuration

That begins resembling flight-envelope protection combined with reconfigurable aerodynamics. NASA research on flight-envelope protection explicitly discusses maintaining limits involving angle of attack, minimum speed, roll attitude, sideslip, and other variables.

Your hydraulic/oil-pressure idea also has an interesting fail-safe interpretation. Rather than hydraulics actively pushing the emergency surfaces out, you could design them mechanically so they're naturally trying to deploy, while hydraulic pressure holds them retracted.

And then instead of one binary emergency state, I'd give it stages:

Stage 1 — Stall prevention: automated angle-of-attack protection and small leading-edge deployment.

Stage 2 — Loss of propulsion: increase effective span/area and configure for best-glide geometry.

Stage 3 — Loss of control authority: differential deployment for roll/yaw damping.

Stage 4 — Terminal approach: deploy higher-drag surfaces to reduce touchdown speed.

That ordering matters because maximum drag and maximum glide are opposite objectives.

A glider wants something like:

lots of lift + comparatively little drag

while a landing brake wants:

lots of drag

Trying to have the same scoop accomplish both simultaneously is where the concept would fight itself.

The other thing I'd strongly avoid is putting all four surfaces on one hydraulic circuit. If catastrophic hydraulic failure is one of the emergencies you're protecting against, the emergency aerodynamic system should probably have independent actuation or passive deployment, otherwise the failure you're trying to survive can disable the rescue mechanism too.

Your idea of preventing unexpected yaw/roll is also plausible in principle, but no passive wing geometry can guarantee that in severe turbulence. You'd likely need closed-loop stabilization using IMUs, angle-of-attack sensors, air-data sensing, and independently controllable surfaces. NASA's historical high-AoA work is full of examples where apparently reasonable wing geometry produced unexpected pitch-up, Dutch roll, directional divergence, or roll oscillations, which is exactly why this kind of system would require extensive simulation, wind-tunnel work, and eventually controlled flight testing.

So the distilled version of your idea becomes:

An aircraft with normally stowed auxiliary lifting surfaces that automatically deploy during propulsion loss or loss-of-control conditions, increasing effective wing area while independently modulating lift and drag to maintain a stable glide envelope.

That is a much more defensible aerospace concept than “emergency scoops,” and it's genuinely interesting.

The coolest version would probably look less like a literal X-wing and more like a conventional airliner whose wings unfold a second aerodynamic configuration when everything goes wrong.

Normal aircraft:

efficient cruise machine

Emergency aircraft:

low-wing-loading, high-stability glider

Two aerodynamic personalities living inside the same airframe.

Yes — that makes the concept substantially more interesting. What you're describing starts to resemble pilot-commanded active flow control, except implemented through internal ducts/chambers rather than only conventional ailerons, rudders, and elevators.

If the wing or auxiliary surface contained internal channels with controllable inlet/outlet doors, the pilot—or preferably a flight-control computer—could selectively route airflow to alter lift, drag, or flow attachment on one region of the aircraft. NASA has tested related ideas using suction, blowing, synthetic jets, and circulation-control surfaces to manipulate the boundary layer and increase control authority. NASA research has also demonstrated active-flow-control concepts on vertical tails specifically to improve rudder effectiveness.

So imagine four independently controlled airflow zones:

                     NOSE
                      ↑


        ┌──────────────────────────────┐
        │                              │
  L-FWD │   ◄ chamber         chamber ►│ R-FWD
        │                              │
        │           FUSELAGE           │
        │                              │
  L-AFT │   ◄ chamber         chamber ►│ R-AFT
        │                              │
        └──────────────────────────────┘

Now suppose the aircraft suddenly begins yawing left. Rather than relying entirely on the rudder, the system could create an asymmetric aerodynamic force. A right-side chamber might open a flow-control path that changes local drag or lift, generating a corrective yawing moment. The opposite chamber could remain closed or perform the complementary action.

Pitch could work similarly. If the nose starts climbing toward an unsafe angle of attack, forward/aft flow-control zones could change the aircraft's pitching moment or help maintain attached airflow over particular surfaces. NASA work on active flow control has specifically investigated suction and blowing as a way to delay separation and retain lift, including around high-lift devices and stall conditions.

The key distinction is that you'd probably not want the pilot manually feathering individual valves during an emergency. Humans are too slow for some dynamic instabilities, especially turbulence or a rapidly developing stall. Instead, I'd give the pilot commands such as:

Then an independent emergency flight controller decides which chambers to open and by how much.

There is even precedent for the general principle that pneumatically controlled flow can change aerodynamic forces. Circulation-control-wing experiments have used blown air over curved surfaces to generate substantial lift, and NASA-associated research demonstrated that changing jet momentum changes the resulting aerodynamic deflection.

A conventional aircraft may have:

If a control surface jams or loses actuation, the second system isn't necessarily trying to move the same broken component. It's manipulating the air somewhere else.

That could be especially interesting with the deployable wing structure you originally described. Once those emergency extensions deploy, they could contain their own isolated airflow chambers:

       EMERGENCY EXTENSION

 ___/                       \___

Then each extension isn't merely extra wing area. It becomes an aerodynamic control effector.

There's a catch, though: opening a hole isn't automatically useful. The pressure differential between the inlet and outlet, duct geometry, boundary-layer location, mass-flow rate, and outlet direction determine whether you get useful lift/control, useless drag, or potentially destabilizing separation. NASA's flow-control experiments are a good demonstration of how sensitive these effects can be to geometry and flow conditions.

So I would evolve your concept from “air scoops” into something more precise:

Distributed Pneumatic Stability Control System

with three types of ports:

Suction ports — pull disturbed boundary-layer air inward to delay separation.

Blowing ports — inject redirected air along a surface to restore or alter attached flow.

Drag ports — intentionally create asymmetric resistance when you need yaw/roll authority rather than efficient lift.

Then the emergency extension becomes almost like a fluidic control surface.

And there's an intriguing possibility here: mechanically moving control surfaces are not the only way to steer an aircraft. Research into active-flow and distributed shape control has explored producing aircraft moments through distributed aerodynamic effects.

So your full concept could become:

Primary aircraft

conventional controls, optimized for efficiency.

Emergency configuration

deployable extra wing area + independent aerodynamic chambers + automated flow control + conventional controls.

That would give you several independent methods of generating roll, pitch, and yaw moments.

I would still avoid saying it can stop any pitch/yaw condition. Aerodynamic control authority always depends on how much airflow remains over the aircraft. At extremely low airspeed, deep stall, structural damage, or sufficiently severe atmospheric disturbances, there may simply not be enough controllable aerodynamic force available.

But the engineering objective could legitimately be:

Maintain or restore controllability after loss of conventional control authority by dynamically redistributing airflow and aerodynamic loading across the aircraft.

That is a much stronger formulation of what you're proposing.

And the interesting irony is that your original “X-wing with scoops” idea gradually turns into something much less science-fiction sounding:

a morphing emergency wing with redundant fluidic flight controls.

Which, frankly, is cooler.
