Yes — that idea becomes much more physically coherent if you frame the glass as a dielectric buffer layer, not as something the plasma itself passes through.

Two panes of ordinary glass placed on either side of a plasma channel can create a kind of dielectric-barrier / capacitive coupling zone. The plasma remains in the gap or channel, while the glass changes how the electric field couples into surrounding conductors.

Conceptually:

electrode / PTEC side
        │
     [glass]
        │
   plasma region
        │
     [glass]
        │
outer conductor / field lattice

The panes can do several useful jobs at once:

Electrical isolation: they prevent direct conductive contact between the plasma and the outer field hardware.

Capacitive coupling: the glass and adjacent conductors behave like part of a capacitor, so changing electric fields can couple across the dielectric.

Charge buffering: surface charge can accumulate on the glass interfaces and locally oppose or reshape the field.

Arc suppression: a dielectric barrier can prevent a discharge from turning immediately into a hard conductive arc.

Field shaping: thickness, spacing, dielectric constant, and electrode geometry affect where the electric field is strongest.

So rather than:

“run the plasma through the glass,”

the more accurate architecture is:

confine plasma between dielectric panes and couple its changing electric field through those panes into surrounding induction structures.

A basic model is similar to a capacitor:

$$ C=\frac{\varepsilon A}{d} $$

where \(A\) is effective area, \(d\) is dielectric thickness, and \(\varepsilon\) is the permittivity of the glass.

Then, if the plasma/electrode voltage changes:

$$ I=C\frac{dV}{dt} $$

which means the outer conductive layer can see displacement current even though there is no direct electrical path through the glass.

That gives you a plausible buffer/induction zone:

PTEC / driver
    ↓
inner electric field
    ↓
glass dielectric
    ↓
plasma interaction zone
    ↓
glass dielectric
    ↓
outer induced charge / field layer
    ↓
mesh / XRM field lattice

The double-pane version is especially interesting because it gives you two separate surface-charge interfaces:

outer layer
   │
[ glass ]
   │
surface charge A
   │
PLASMA
   │
surface charge B
   │
[ glass ]
   │
outer layer

Those two surfaces can respond differently in time, so the system can act less like one raw discharge and more like a layered field-conditioning device.

That connects very naturally to your earlier PTEC concept:

PTEC A ─────┐
            ├─ inner field / plasma zone
PTEC B ─────┘
                 │
            glass barriers
                 │
         induced outer layer
                 │
          shield mesh / bus

The thing worth testing would be whether the glass-buffered plasma structure gives you better:

field uniformity,
arc suppression,
induced outer-surface voltage,
transient storage,
thermal isolation,
or field persistence

than the same plasma/electrode setup without the dielectric panes.

The key measurements would be \(V\), \(I\), surface charge, electric-field strength, plasma stability, pane temperature, and induced voltage on the outer mesh.

So yes — what you're describing can be turned into a real low-scale PoC as a dual-dielectric plasma induction buffer.

And that is a stronger formulation than simply saying “plasma between glass.” It gives the glass an explicit job: separate, store, shape, and transfer the field without direct conduction.
