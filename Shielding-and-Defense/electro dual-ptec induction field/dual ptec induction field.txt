Document Type: Low-voltage electromagnetic field-generation proof of concept

One-Line Doctrine

Two independently controlled PTEC sources feed a shared conductive loop or mesh to establish a distributed current path, producing a measurable electromagnetic field whose geometry and temporal behavior can be controlled through conductor layout, current magnitude, and source phase.

1. Executive Premise

A PTEC does not need to independently operate an entire field-generating structure.

Two PTECs can instead be positioned as distributed electrical sources connected through a common conductive pathway. Current flowing through that pathway produces an associated magnetic field.

The proposed architecture is:

        PTEC A                  PTEC B
           │                       │
           ▼                       ▼
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
     │                                 │
     │      conductive wire/mesh       │
     │                                 │
     │             EM                  │
     │         FIELD REGION            │
     │                                 │
     └─────────────────────────────────┘

The PoC asks a straightforward question:

Can two PTECs cooperatively energize a distributed conductor and establish a stable, measurable, controllable electromagnetic field across a useful enclosed region?

The first experiment requires neither plasma nor high voltage.

2. Physical Basis

Current flowing through a conductor generates a magnetic field.

For a simple long conductor, an approximate relationship is:

$$ B=\frac{\mu I}{2\pi r} $$

while coil-like structures are more usefully characterized through their ampere-turns:

$$ B\propto NI $$

where:

\(B\) = magnetic flux density,
\(I\) = current,
\(N\) = effective number of turns,
\(\mu\) = magnetic permeability,
\(r\) = measurement distance.

The PoC therefore does not depend upon an assumed exotic field mechanism.

Its first objective is ordinary electromagnetic characterization.

3. Two-PTEC Architecture

Rather than directly tying two raw PTEC outputs together, each source should have its own conditioning and isolation stage.

 PTEC A                            PTEC B
    │                                 │
    ▼                                 ▼
DC CONDITIONER                  DC CONDITIONER
    │                                 │
CURRENT CONTROL                 CURRENT CONTROL
    │                                 │
ISOLATION                       ISOLATION
    │                                 │
    └───────────┐       ┌─────────────┘
                ▼       ▼
             FIELD CONDUCTOR

This prevents one PTEC from unintentionally driving current backward through the other.

It also makes each PTEC independently measurable.

4. Distributed Conductor

The central experimental element is the conductive structure connecting the two energy sources.

Three geometries should be compared.

Configuration A — Straight Conductors

Establishes the simplest electromagnetic baseline.

Configuration B — Conductive Mesh

Distributes current spatially over a larger surface.

This may produce a broader but less concentrated field pattern.

Configuration C — Patterned Coil / Loop

Intentionally routes current around the desired field region.

This should provide a stronger magnetic field per unit current than an arbitrary mesh when the geometry reinforces rather than cancels neighboring contributions.

This comparison is important because the PoC should determine whether mesh geometry itself provides an advantage.

5. Resistance Question

The original concept proposes a low-voltage, high-resistance conductor.

That configuration should be tested, but resistance should become an experimental variable rather than an assumed advantage.

From Ohm's law:

$$ I=\frac{V}{R} $$

and resistive heating follows:

$$ P_{heat}=I^2R. $$

Increasing resistance generally reduces available current for a fixed voltage while converting part of the supplied electrical energy into heat.

Therefore the PoC should compare:

HIGH-R PATH
    vs.
MEDIUM-R PATH
    vs.
LOW-R PATH

and measure both electromagnetic output and thermal loss.

That lets the experiment decide which configuration actually serves the architecture.

6. Static Field Mode

The simplest operating mode drives both sources steadily.

PTEC A ───────►
               FIELD LOOP
PTEC B ───────►

Measure:

$$ I_A,\ I_B,\ V_A,\ V_B $$

along with:

$$ B(x,y,z). $$

This produces a spatial map of the resulting magnetic field.

The first success criterion is simply:

A repeatable magnetic field exists and changes predictably with PTEC current.

7. Differential Drive Mode

The two PTECs can then be operated independently.

For example:

$$ I_A>I_B $$

followed by:

$$ I_A<I_B. $$

If the conductor is appropriately segmented, changing this relationship can shift the resulting field distribution.

The architecture therefore becomes more than two sources connected in parallel.

It becomes a two-node field controller.

8. Pulsed Field Mode

The PTECs can supply a DC bus while switching electronics pulse the field conductors.

Conceptually:

PTEC
 ↓
DC BUS
 ↓
SWITCHING STAGE
 ↓
FIELD CONDUCTOR
 ↓
B(t)

This separates PTEC generation from field modulation.

That distinction is useful because the PTEC does not itself need to change output extremely rapidly.

9. Phase-Controlled Mode

A more advanced experiment divides the conductor into two independently driven field sections.

Drive them as:

$$ I_A(t)=I_0\sin(\omega t) $$

and

$$ I_B(t)=I_0\sin(\omega t+\phi). $$

The phase difference,

$$ \phi, $$

becomes another control parameter.

Test:

$$ 0^\circ,\quad90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ. $$

The objective is to determine whether the resulting field can be shifted or made to exhibit a controlled traveling pattern.

13. Field Persistence Experiment

An especially useful test is determining what happens when one PTEC is removed.

Start with:

A + B → FIELD

then:

A only → FIELD

then:

B only → FIELD

then:

A + B → FIELD

Measure how quickly the field changes and whether any intermediate storage elements alter that transition.

Adding capacitive or inductive storage later gives:

PTEC A ─┐
        ├── BUS ── STORAGE ── FIELD NETWORK
PTEC B ─┘

Now generation, storage, and field production become separate layers.

15. Failure Modes

The first concerns are mundane but important.

Backfeed: one PTEC may drive the other unless the sources are appropriately isolated.

Heating: excessive conductor resistance can turn intended field power into heat.

Current concentration: mesh junctions can develop local hotspots.

Field cancellation: poorly designed conductor geometry can cause neighboring currents to produce opposing magnetic fields.

Switching transients: rapidly switched inductive loads can generate voltage spikes.

Measurement contamination: nearby ferromagnetic objects, power supplies, wiring, and sensors can distort the measured field.

These should all be characterized before interpreting unusual measurements.

16. Success Criteria

The initial PoC succeeds if:

both PTECs can feed the common architecture without uncontrolled backfeeding;
the resulting magnetic field is repeatable;
field strength changes predictably with current;
field geometry can be altered through conductor geometry;
dual-source operation provides a measurable advantage over one source;
thermal losses can be quantified;
differential or phased operation produces controllable spatial/temporal field behavior;
all measured energy remains consistent with the electrical input and stored-energy changes.

The experiment does not need to produce a spectacular field to succeed.

It needs to establish a controllable relationship between:

$$ \boxed{ \text{PTEC power} \rightarrow \text{distributed current} \rightarrow \text{field geometry} } $$
17. Conclusion

The Dual-PTEC Distributed EM Induction Field PoC proposes that two PTEC sources can cooperate through a shared conductive lattice rather than functioning only as independent power units.

The PTECs provide energy.

The conductor provides geometry.

Current establishes the magnetic field.

Segmentation provides spatial control.

Switching provides temporal control.

Phase provides motion.

The resulting architecture becomes a small-scale experimental precursor to the larger distributed XRM field-manifold concept.

Final Doctrine Statement

Dual-PTEC Distributed EM Induction Field is a two-source electromagnetic architecture in which independently conditioned PTEC modules energize a shared conductive loop, mesh, or patterned field lattice to create a measurable distributed magnetic field whose intensity, geometry, and temporal behavior can be controlled through current, resistance, conductor topology, switching, and phase.
