The strongest applications would be situations where downtime, maintainability, fault isolation, or modular expansion matter enough to justify some loss in packing density.

Grid and stationary energy storage — probably the strongest initial application. A containerized EFRS installation could contain hundreds or thousands of cartridges. When individual cartridges deteriorate, technicians could isolate and replace them instead of retiring an entire module. It could also allow capacity to be expanded incrementally. This environment is forgiving of the extra mass associated with cartridge housings, connectors, coolant manifolds, and isolation hardware.
Data centers, hospitals and critical infrastructure. An EFRS bank could operate as modular UPS/storage infrastructure. Individually monitored cartridges create the possibility of graceful degradation: identify a failing cartridge → isolate it → continue operating the remaining array → replace it during maintenance.
Electric vehicles. Instead of treating the traction battery as one enormous component, a vehicle could have several EFRS cores. The potentially attractive feature isn't magical range; it's maintenance. A degraded section could theoretically be serviced at cartridge or core level rather than automatically replacing a complete traction pack. The challenge is that conventional EV packs are extremely space- and mass-sensitive, so EFRS would have to prove that serviceability compensates for its packaging overhead.
Commercial trucks, buses and fleet vehicles. This might actually be a better automotive target than passenger cars. Fleet operators care intensely about downtime and lifecycle cost. A modular storage system could allow scheduled replacement of aging cartridges and potentially let different vehicle variants use the same basic core with different cartridge counts.

If one branch develops an electrical fault, the power-management system could potentially isolate that branch while preserving remaining capability. Aerospace use would face extremely demanding certification, mass, vibration, thermal-runaway and reliability requirements, however.

Ships and submarines. Marine installations have enough volume that a serviceable modular battery architecture becomes more attractive. Multiple EFRS banks could support propulsion, hotel loads, emergency systems and peak loads while allowing sections of the storage plant to be isolated for maintenance.
Remote installations and microgrids. Think research stations, telecommunications sites, islands, mines and remote industrial facilities. Instead of transporting an enormous replacement battery assembly, standardized cartridges could potentially be transported individually.
Robotics and industrial equipment. Forklifts, autonomous mining vehicles, warehouse robots and construction equipment could use standardized cartridges or cores. The same physical energy architecture could potentially scale across different machines.
Renewable-energy buffering. Solar and wind installations generate variable power. EFRS banks could absorb excess generation and return it later:
Solar/Wind→EFRS→Grid/Load

The modular architecture becomes especially interesting because storage installations tend to grow and undergo maintenance over many years.

Hybrid battery/supercapacitor systems. This might become one of the more interesting derivatives of your idea. The cartridge standard doesn't necessarily require every cartridge to contain identical storage technology. You could conceptually have high-energy and high-power cartridges sharing a managed DC architecture:
Energy cartridges+Power cartridges→Common DC bus
	​


The high-power modules handle acceleration or regenerative spikes while the high-energy modules handle sustained operation. That could reduce stress on the primary cells, although the economics and control architecture would need testing.

There is also a potentially important second-order application: battery remanufacturing. Imagine a 500-cartridge stationary system reaching 10 years of operation. Instead of declaring the entire pack end-of-life, diagnostics might identify 37 badly degraded cartridges. Those could be replaced while healthy cartridges remain in service. Older but functional cartridges could potentially be characterized and reassigned to less demanding stationary applications.

That leads to what I think is the most compelling long-term EFRS idea: standardization.

Think about what happened with standardized computing hardware. The interface became relatively stable while the devices behind it improved. An analogous EFRS specification could define mechanical dimensions, terminals, communications, cooling interfaces, isolation requirements and electrical operating envelopes.
